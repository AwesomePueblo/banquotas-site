Faith & Scripture

Remnant Fellowship: Biblical Foundations

The goal is not argument. It is to let scripture speak.

Introduction

Remnant Fellowship is frequently mischaracterized — defined in the public mind by tragedy rather than by its theology. This document draws from scripture to show that Remnant's core teachings are not innovations but a return to what the Bible plainly says: that God is supreme, that Jesus is his Son, and that faith without genuine transformation is not faith at all.

The goal is not argument. It is to let scripture speak.

God Is One: The Foundation of the Torah

The most foundational declaration of scripture is found in the Torah — the first five books of the Old Testament: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4, NIV 1984). This absolute monotheism is not qualified or complicated. God is singular, supreme, and without equal.

This is not a starting point the Remnant invented. It is the bedrock of the entire Old Testament and the consistent teaching of Jesus himself. When asked which commandment was greatest, Jesus quoted Deuteronomy 6:4 directly (Mark 12:29, NIV 1984) and placed love of God above all else. The oneness of God is not a secondary doctrine. It is the foundation on which everything else rests.

Jesus as the Son of God

Remnant teaches that Jesus is the Son of God — not God incarnate, not a second person of a co-equal Trinity. This is precisely what Jesus himself said. When accused of claiming to be God, he corrected the charge: "Do you say of him whom the Father has sanctified and sent into the world, 'You are blaspheming,' because I said, 'I am the Son of God'?" (John 10:36, NIV 1984).

Jesus consistently directed attention upward, toward the Father. "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18, NIV 1984). He prayed to the Father. He said the Father was greater than he was (John 14:28, NIV 1984). He did not accept worship that belonged to God alone.

The doctrine of the Trinity was formalized at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD — three centuries after Jesus lived. The Remnant's position is not a departure from historic Christianity. It is a return to what Jesus and his earliest followers actually said.

Salvation as Doing the Will of God

Jesus was explicit: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 7:21, NIV 1984). Salvation in the Remnant framework is not a transaction — a prayer said, a box checked. It is a life genuinely surrendered to God's will.

This is not a novel teaching. It is the plain reading of the Sermon on the Mount. It is what James meant when he wrote that faith without works is dead (James 2:17, NIV 1984). It is what John meant when he wrote that those who say they know God but do not keep his commandments are liars (1 John 2:4, NIV 1984).

The Remnant's emphasis on actual obedience — on lives that change — is not legalism. It is taking Jesus at his word.

Death to Self: The Path Jesus Modeled

Jesus did not merely teach surrender to the Father's will. He demonstrated it, at cost, all the way to his death. His life is the pattern every follower is called to imitate.

"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me" (Luke 9:23, NIV 1984). The cross is not only something Jesus carried. It is the shape of the life he calls his followers into — daily death to self, daily choosing the Father's will over their own.

Gwen Shamblin Lara understood that this principle applied to the most ordinary and daily human struggle: appetite. If a person cannot surrender what they eat to God, how can they surrender anything harder? The body became a classroom for learning obedience. That is a deeply biblical instinct — Paul uses the same logic in 1 Corinthians 9:27 (NIV 1984).

The False Religion of Grace Without Transformation

One of the Remnant's sharpest critiques of mainstream Christianity is that grace has been turned into a license — freedom to sin rather than freedom from sin. This is not a new concern. Paul addressed it directly: "Are we to continue in sin that grace may abound? By no means!" (Romans 6:1–2, NIV 1984).

A faith that does not change how a person lives is not the faith of the New Testament. Jesus warned against it. James warned against it. John warned against it. The idea that a person can confess belief and live however they choose, covered by forgiveness, is a corruption of the gospel — not its fullest expression.

The Remnant's insistence that real faith produces real change is not harshness. It is fidelity to what the text actually says.

Conclusion

The Remnant Fellowship holds a demanding theology — but it is not an invented one. Its core convictions are drawn from the plain reading of scripture: that God is one, that Jesus is his Son who showed us how to live, and that genuine faith transforms the life of the believer from the inside out.

People whose lives have actually changed are the most credible argument any community can offer. The Remnant has thousands of them. That story deserves to be told on its own terms — not through the lens of tragedy, but through the lens of what the teaching actually is and what it has actually done in real lives.

The Bible called for this kind of faith long before the Remnant did. That is the point.
